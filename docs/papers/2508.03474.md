# 2508.03474 — Arbitrage in Prediction Markets (Polymarket)

All 36 pages read (main text pp. 1–21, references pp. 21–24, appendices A–I pp. 24–36). Page numbers below are the printed PDF page numbers.

## 1. Citation

- **Title:** Unravelling the Probabilistic Forest: Arbitrage in Prediction Markets
- **Authors:** Oriol Saguillo, Vahid Ghafouri, Lucianna Kiffer, Guillermo Suarez-Tangil (IMDEA Networks; Oxford Internet Institute)
- **Year:** 2025 (v1 dated 5 Aug 2025; acknowledges AFT reviewers)
- **arXiv:** 2508.03474v1 [cs.CR]

## 2. Summary

The paper measures how often Polymarket prices break basic probability rules, and how much money traders made from it, for markets that resolved 1 Apr 2024 – 1 Apr 2025. It defines two kinds of arbitrage. **Market Rebalancing Arbitrage** happens inside one market or condition, when YES prices across outcomes that cannot both happen sum to something other than $1. **Combinatorial Arbitrage** happens across two logically linked markets, for example "who wins state X" against "margin in state X". Comparing every pair of markets naively costs O(2^(n+m)), so the authors cut the search space by grouping markets by topic and end date, then use an LLM (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B) to list every valid joint resolution of the paired markets. They then detect opportunities in on-chain VWAP prices and match them to the wallets that took them. Their estimate is about **$40M in realized arbitrage profit** (p. 1; $39,587,585.02 on p. 21). Most of it is single-market rebalancing. Cross-market arbitrage was rare and small.

## 3. Data & method

- **Market metadata:** Polymarket API through py-clob-client. 8,659 single-condition markets plus 1,578 NegRisk (multi-condition) markets holding 8,559 conditions, for 17,218 conditions total (p. 10). End dates within a market were inconsistent, so the authors use the modal `end_date_iso`, breaking ties by taking the latest date (p. 10).
- **Topic tagging:** Linq-Embed-Mistral embeddings, assigned to Polymarket's 7 categories by cosine similarity. A hand check of 100 samples gave 92% accuracy (pp. 10–11).
- **Trades:** Polygon logs from the Conditional Token contract (OrderFilled, PositionSplit, PositionsMerge), pulled through Alchemy for blocks from 1 Jan 2024 to 1 Apr 2025 (pp. 11–12). The data holds 86,620,143 bids, with mean $135.616, median $8.289, and max $2,478,476.448 (p. 35, Fig. 19).
- **Dependency detection:** Pairs are only compared when they share a topic and an end date. Markets with more than 4 conditions are cut to the top 4 by volume plus an "OR-of-the-rest" 5th condition, which the authors say keeps over 90% of liquidity (p. 14, App. C p. 27). The LLM returns a JSON list of all valid truth-value vectors (prompt on p. 26). Validity checks: (i) the JSON parses, (ii) exactly one true value per market, (iii) at most n+m vectors (pp. 13–14).
- **Opportunity detection:** VWAP is computed per position over one block. The last price is carried forward for up to 5K blocks (about 2.5 h) and then set to 0. Times when any position is above $0.95 are excluded (p. 15). The profit threshold is stated inconsistently: the text says "at least $0.05 on the dollar" (p. 15), while the Fig. 5 caption uses |1−VWAP sum| > 0.02 and Figs. 6 and 14 mention a "2 cents bound" (pp. 15, 16, 32).
- **Realized arbitrage:** Bids are grouped per wallet within T = 950 blocks (about 1 h), which covers 75% of placement-to-execution delays. Bids under $2 are dropped. **Fees are ignored because "Polymarket currently does not charge per trade"** (p. 18).

## 4. Key quantitative findings

- LLM single-market check on 128 Politics NegRisk markets (2024-11-05): 4 returned no valid JSON, and **101 (81.45%) passed every check**. Failures were blamed on too many conditions per prompt (p. 13).
- Non-US-election pairs: 2,267 checked. 30 returned no JSON, 203 failed the checks, 2,033 were independent, and 1 was dependent, though it did not meet the strict definition (p. 14, Table 3 p. 27).
- US-election pairs (128 NegRisk + 177 Single markets): 46,360 pairs. 353 returned no JSON, 4,374 were invalid, 40,057 were independent, and 1,576 were dependent. The subset checker flagged 374 (4 NegRisk–NegRisk, 94 Single–Single, 276 NegRisk–Single). **Manual review confirmed only 13 (11 NR–NR, 2 NR–Single)** (p. 14). That is a precision of about 3.5% on checker output (13/374, my arithmetic). Eight ambiguous markets produced 1,469 invalid pairs (p. 14, Table 5 p. 28).
- Single-condition arbitrage: **7,051 of about 17.2K conditions** had at least one opportunity (2,628 NegRisk, 4,423 Single). All were *long*, meaning YES+NO < 1 (pp. 15–17). The median "sum of conditions (profit per dollar)" was around $0.60 across topics (p. 17).
- NegRisk rebalancing: **662 of 1,578** NegRisk markets had at least one opportunity, averaging around 100 per market. The average max opportunity was about 40¢/$ for both long and short (p. 17).
- Cross-market: Pair 8 had 0 opportunities, the median for the others was 8, and pairs 2, 1, and 4 had 72, 176, and 6,630. Average max profit was around $100, which points to total token volume under 2K. These occurred "largely during lower liquidity moments" (p. 18).
- Realized single-condition profit: buy-below-$1 **$5,899,287.427**, sell-above-$1 **$4,682,074.77** (p. 19). About 1% of estimated US-election opportunities were exploited (Fig. 10 caption, p. 19). The largest single trade was $58,983.36, by @Tutaaa91 buying both YES and NO under $0.02 each (p. 19).
- Realized NegRisk/market profit: buy YES **$11,092,286.31**, sell YES $612,188.83, sell NO $4,264.33, buy NO **$17,307,113.81** (p. 20).
- Realized cross-market profit (5 of 11 pairs): pair 2 $60,236.71, pair 4 $18,472.31, pair 1 $15,818.53, pair 3 $629.16 (p. 20). That sums to about $95K, compared with $39.6M overall.
- Total: **$39,587,585.02** assuming ε = $1/trade. The top wallet made $2,009,631.76 over 4,049 transactions (Table 1, p. 21). Profit is heavily concentrated, and the top accounts look like bots (Fig. 12, p. 20).
- Over 90% of a multi-condition market's liquidity sits in its top 4 conditions (App. C, p. 27).

## 5. Microstructure, calibration, fees, biases, LLMs, leakage

- **Microstructure:** Polymarket runs a hybrid CLOB with off-chain matching and on-chain settlement. Complementary buys mint YES/NO pairs (PositionSplit) and complementary sells burn them (PositionsMerge) (p. 5). Arbitrage is non-atomic, so legs can fail (pp. 3, 8). Market Descriptors include `maker_base_fee`, `taker_base_fee` (both 0 in the example), `minimum_tick_size` 0.001, and `minimum_order_size` 5.0 (p. 25).
- **Fees:** Ignored, because Polymarket charged no per-trade fee at the time (p. 18). **This does not carry over to Kalshi**, which charges trading fees.
- **Calibration / favorite-longshot bias:** Not tested directly. The closest evidence is that "buy NO" was the most profitable realized strategy at $17.3M, and Polymarket publicly noted accounts profiting just by buying NO (p. 20). That fits overpriced longshot YES in multi-outcome markets, but the paper does not frame it as an FLB test. Fig. 8 says Sports markets "are often overvalued" (p. 17).
- **Resolution risk:** UMA oracle disputes can produce outcomes that differ from reality. Example: the 2024 FIDE Blitz market, where two winners existed but only one could be marked True (p. 5).
- **LLM use:** The LLM is used for logical-dependency extraction, not forecasting. Failure modes: reasoning loops that print all 2^(n+m) assignments (p. 14, fn. 9), confusing popular vote with electoral college or Senate with House (p. 14), assuming "Democrat" means "Kamala" (28 pairs, Table 11 p. 32), and ambiguous "largest margin" or overlapping-bucket wording (Table 5, p. 28).
- **Leakage controls:** No lookahead or leakage controls are discussed, because the LLM never predicts outcomes. The only time-related filter is the >$0.95 exclusion.

## 6. Implications for our system

**Backtest variations**
1. **Coherence baseline.** Before scoring our forecaster against market price, compute per-event sum(YES) deviation on Kalshi multi-outcome events. If the market itself is incoherent, renormalize it first, both proportionally and with a longshot-shrink, and test whether our edge survives against the renormalized market rather than the raw price.
2. **Buy-NO-on-longshots strategy** as an FLB test: in multi-outcome political events, buy NO on every outcome with YES below some threshold. Sweep thresholds of 5/10/15¢ and net out Kalshi fees per contract at each price level. The paper's $17.3M buy-NO result (p. 20) is the prior to beat.
3. **Window sensitivity.** The paper found that 1-block versus 100-block price averaging changes measured arbitrage a lot (Fig. 15, p. 33). Run our calibration backtests at several snapshot granularities (last trade, 1-min mid, 1-h VWAP) and report how much each changes the measured edge.
4. **Liquidity-conditioned edge.** Cross-market mispricings appeared mostly in low-liquidity moments, with about $100 max (p. 18). Stratify our edge by depth and spread at signal time and drop signals where the fillable size after fees is under a set dollar floor.
5. **Bucket/margin consistency checks** for Kalshi margin-bucket markets against winner markets (the p. 29–30 pair types): flag when P(winner) ≠ sum of P(that party's margin buckets).

**Retrieval / KB design**
- Tag every market with **(topic, resolution date)** and build the retrieval candidate set from those keys. That is the paper's main pruning step (p. 11).
- Put **explicit resolution criteria** in each markdown knowledge file, such as popular vote vs EC, chamber, candidate vs party, and inclusive/exclusive bucket edges. The LLM errors on pp. 14 and 28 all came from this kind of ambiguity.
- Cap the outcomes per LLM prompt at 5 or fewer, collapsing the tail into an "other" bucket (p. 14). Validate structured output with schema checks for exactly one true outcome and normalized probabilities, and retry on failure. Expect around 19% failures on large prompts (p. 13).
- Don't treat LLM-flagged dependencies as ground truth. Only 13 of 374 survived manual review (p. 14).

**Leakage/lookahead controls (ours, not from the paper)**
- Snapshot features and knowledge files with as-of timestamps, and use the market `end_date` only as metadata, never as an outcome signal.
- Exclude periods after a price crosses 95¢ when measuring pre-resolution calibration (the paper's filter, p. 15). This also limits leakage from markets that are effectively resolved.

**Participant-sophistication features**
- **Wallet concentration:** profit is extremely skewed. The top 10 wallets made $0.38M–$2.0M each (Table 1, p. 21). On Kalshi, wallet IDs aren't available, so use proxies: trade-size distribution tails, bursty high-count fills, and how quickly incoherence closes.
- **Time-to-close of incoherence** (sum(YES) − 1 reverting toward 0) works as a market-level sophistication metric. Slow reversion suggests a retail-dominated, mispriced market.
- **Placement-to-execution delay** (Fig. 19, p. 35): our analogue is the lag between a news event and a price move.

## 7. Limitations / caveats

- The data is Polymarket on Polygon only, with no fees, a crypto-settled user base, and UMA resolution. Results carry over to Kalshi (fees, CFTC-regulated, centralized resolution) only as qualitative priors.
- VWAP over executed trades understates the profit actually available (Fig. 5 caption, p. 15), and it misses the order-book state when nothing traded. The "maximum profit" figures assume all available tokens could be bought, so they are upper bounds (p. 17).
- The profit threshold is stated inconsistently (5¢ vs 2¢; pp. 15, 16, 32). The $40M total depends on an ε = $1 per-trade assumption (p. 21).
- Dependency detection covers only market pairs, only those with the same topic and end date, and only strict partition dependencies. Weaker temporal dependencies, like semifinal → final, are out of scope (p. 21).
- The LLM validation set is small (128 markets), and the 13 valid pairs came from manual review with no inter-rater measure reported.
- There is no calibration analysis, no forecasting evaluation, and no explicit favorite-longshot test.
