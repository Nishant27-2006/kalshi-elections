# 2601.01706: Semantic Non-Fungibility and Violations of the Law of One Price in Prediction Markets

## 1. Citation

- **Title:** Semantic Non-Fungibility and Violations of the Law of One Price in Prediction Markets (running head: "Liquidity Fragmentation in Prediction Markets")
- **Authors:** Jonas Gebele, Florian Matthes (Technical University of Munich)
- **Year:** 2026 (arXiv v1, 5 Jan 2026)
- **arXiv:** 2601.01706v1 [cs.CE]
- **Length:** 39 pages: 25 body, references, appendices A–I. All pages were read.

## 2. Summary

The paper argues that prediction-market venues have no shared, machine-verifiable event identity. Because of that, economically equivalent contracts on different venues cannot be netted, and their prices break the Law of One Price. The authors build an LLM + embedding pipeline that aligns markets across 10 venues (102,275 events, 2018 to Aug 2025). About 6% of events have at least one cross-platform semantic relation (equivalent, subset, or negative-risk partition). Among equivalent markets, execution-aware price gaps persist: the median gap is typically 2–4% from parity, even in the most liquid constructions. The authors attribute this to structural frictions (capital locked until resolution, differing resolution rules, regulatory segmentation) and not to disagreement about information. A 2024 presidential-election case study shows a persistent Kalshi–Polymarket spread of roughly $0.03, reaching $0.07, before election night. The cause is mainly that the two venues resolve on different events: media call versus inauguration.

## 3. Data & method

- **Venues (p.11, Table G p.37):** Polymarket (29,651 events), Kalshi (59,176), Futuur (43,910), Omen (12,846), Limitless (5,560), PredictIt (4,381), Myriad (3,198), Augur v1 (2,569), Seer (878), Truemarkets (562). Kalshi data come from the Kalshi Trade API v2. PredictIt price data were available only on request.
- **Inclusion (p.11):** a venue needs at least 50 markets with lifetime volume above $500. Play-money venues (Metaculus, Manifold) and venues whose prices replicate sportsbook odds are excluded. **Sports markets are excluded.** Markets with zero volume, missing timestamps, or inconsistent records are removed. Data run through Aug 15–22, 2025.
- **Prices (p.11):** CLOB venues use the reported mid-quote. AMM prices are reconstructed from on-chain reserves. Timestamps are in UTC.
- **Formal model (pp.7–10):** a one-sided friction δ(m) covers spread, fees, gas, tick size, and slippage. The paper gives no-arbitrage bounds for single-market YES+NO parity, cross-platform parity (1−Δ ≤ p_Y(m_i)+p_N(m_j) ≤ 1+Δ), negative-risk sums (Σ(p_Y+δ) ≥ 1), cross-platform partitions over an "atomic outcome space" Ω, and subset/superset relations (f(m_s) ⊂ f(m_S)).
- **Matching pipeline (pp.12–15):**
  1. Structural filter: different platform, same LLM-assigned category (20 categories; prompt in App. B), overlapping validity windows. This leaves about 10^4 candidates per market (p.13).
  2. Embedding retrieval: OpenAI `text-embedding-3-large` (3072-d) over title, description, outcome labels, and resolution metadata, keeping the top k=20 by cosine similarity (p.13).
  3. Two LLM passes: a high-recall plausibility filter (App. D), then structured YES-region comparison for equivalence (App. E) and subset (App. F) relations.
  - Negative-risk partitions use 2–4 outcome substitutions, and only the top 1,000 permutations by volume are evaluated (p.15).
- **Validation (p.15):** 1,000 stratified pairs, where agreement was "effectively perfect" but the sample was mostly true negatives. Another 1,000 verification-stage pairs gave κ = 0.94. Each regime had **one** human annotator.
- **Price analysis (p.19, App. I p.39):** 7 of 10 venues. Fee assumptions: Kalshi 1.5% plus $0.01 spread ("dynamic fee schedule; conservatively fixed at 1.5%"), Polymarket CLOB 0% plus $0.01, Polymarket CPMM 2%, Futuur 6% plus $0.01, Omen 2%, Myriad 0–2%, Truemarkets 0.8%, Limitless 1.5%.

## 5. Relevant themes

- **Microstructure (pp.6, 11, 23):** a CLOB mid-quote "reflect[s] order-book microstructure rather than a continuous implied probability" (p.6). Tick size, fee schedule, and venue design can keep prices apart even when beliefs are identical.
- **Fees (App. I p.39):** Kalshi was modeled at a fixed 1.5% because its fee schedule is dynamic. That is a simplification. Kalshi's actual fee depends on price (it is largest near 50¢), so 1.5% flat is not exact. Our system should model Kalshi's real fee formula.
- **Resolution semantics (pp.5–6, 22):** two examples show that textually identical contracts can differ. NYC temperature resolves at Central Park on Kalshi and at LaGuardia on Polymarket. Trump-YES resolves on inauguration for Kalshi and Myriad but on media call for Polymarket and Limitless. The NYC station case fooled both the LLM pipeline and the human annotator (p.16).
- **Calibration and favorite-longshot bias:** **not studied.** The paper has no calibration curves, no Brier or log-loss scores, and no test of the favorite-longshot bias. Its only probability claim is the standard p_Y ≈ E[X] (p.4).
- **LLM forecasting:** none. LLMs are used only for categorization and semantic matching.
- **Retrieval/leakage:** the retrieval design (embedding top-k followed by LLM verification) is relevant. There is no discussion of lookahead or leakage, since the task is matching, not forecasting.
- **Participant sophistication:** this is only indirect. Partially segmented trader populations (US-only Kalshi vs. Polymarket) are cited as one cause of divergence (p.23). There is no trade-level or trader-level analysis.

## 6. Implications for our system

**Backtest variations**
1. **Cross-venue price as a feature/benchmark.** Use the Polymarket (and PredictIt) price for the equivalent contract, net of fees, as an input or as a second baseline. Test whether gaps of 2–4% (p.20) predict where Kalshi converges or resolves. Compare against Kalshi price alone using Brier/log-loss after fees.
2. **Resolution-mismatch adjustment.** For Kalshi political markets, get the resolution trigger (certification, inauguration, AP call) from the rules text. Test whether the forecast should differ from Polymarket's price by P(call ≠ final outcome) plus a time-value or lockup term. The p.23 spread of $0.03–$0.07 is an upper reference.
3. **Horizon-normalized edge.** The paper shows that APY is driven by time to resolution (p.21). Report edge both per contract and annualized, and require a minimum absolute edge above the true Kalshi fee. A large annualized edge on a 1-day market can come from a small absolute mispricing.
4. **Fee realism.** Run the backtest under (a) Kalshi's actual price-dependent fee, (b) the paper's flat 1.5%, and (c) zero fee, which applied to the 2024 presidential market (p.23). Report how much the edge depends on the fee assumption.
5. **Persistence filter.** Count a mispricing signal only if it lasts ≥30 min (p.23) or ≥1 h (p.20). This screens out single quote ticks.
6. **Logical-consistency checks.** Within Kalshi, check negative-risk sums (Σ p_Y across mutually exclusive outcomes) and subset monotonicity, for example "party wins presidency" ≥ "candidate X wins" (p.10). Use violations as a signal or as a constraint that forecasts must satisfy.

**Retrieval design lessons**
- Keep the two-stage design: dense retrieval (k≈20) then LLM verification. Embeddings separate related from unrelated markets but cannot tell equivalent from subset (μ 0.356 vs 0.459, overlapping distributions; p.14).
- Embed resolution metadata (rules, source, cutoff), not just the title (p.13). Pre-filter by category and time-window overlap (p.12–13).
- Do not reuse the paper's "benefit of the doubt" equivalence prompt (App. E, p.33) as-is. It treats unnamed data sources as equivalent, which is the kind of choice that missed the NYC station case. For trading, use a strict resolution-diff step.
- Note a small inconsistency: the text says top k=20 holds 99.9% (p.13), while the App. C caption says fewer than 1 in 1,000 relations fall outside top **k=16** (p.31).

**Leakage/lookahead controls (our additions; the paper does not address these)**
- Embedding and LLM matching over full market histories uses each market's final description and rules. Match on versions of the rules snapshotted at time t.
- Their "max deviation" uses the full joint lifetime (p.20), which is ex post. In a backtest, compute only rolling, as-of-t metrics.
- Survivorship: they exclude zero-volume markets and markets with missing timestamps (p.11). Make sure our universe includes markets that died or were voided.

**Participant-sophistication features**
- Venue segmentation is a proxy: Kalshi is US-only (p.23). Relative lead or lag versus Polymarket around news, as in Fig. 9's election-night jump, can feed a news-reaction-speed feature.
- Bimodal arbitrage presence (p.21) suggests a market-level feature: the fraction of time the Kalshi–Polymarket gap exceeds fees, as a measure of how actively arbitrageurs discipline that market.
- Tick size of $0.01 on Kalshi vs. $0.001 on Polymarket (p.23) puts a floor on how informative Kalshi's quoted spread can be near the bounds.

## 7. Limitations / caveats

- There is no calibration, accuracy, or favorite-longshot analysis. The paper is about price consistency across venues, not about forecasting skill.
- Fee and spread assumptions are standardized and "conservative" (App. I). Kalshi's dynamic fee is flattened to 1.5%. Slippage is assumed negligible for the election market (p.23, fn. 7). Mid-quotes are used, not executable prices.
- Price analysis covers only 7 of 10 venues (p.19). Sports markets are excluded entirely (p.11).
- Each human-validation regime used a single annotator, and the first sample was mostly negatives (p.15, fn. 5).
- Matching relies on proprietary LLMs and OpenAI embeddings at about 200M tokens (p.16). The specific chat model is not named.
- The 1,218.66% strategy return (p.22) is a hypothetical, fully mechanical simulation of 15 trades. It has no capacity, counterparty, or regulatory-access constraints, and it assumes one actor can hold positions on all venues (p.7, assumption iii).
- The k=20 vs. k=16 discrepancy (p.13 vs. p.31) is unexplained.
- The dataset is promised "upon publication" and was not yet released in v1 (p.11).
